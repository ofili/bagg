# Bagg — Product Definition and Product Vision

**Document type:** Product Definition  
**Status:** Working product vision and scope baseline  
**Product:** Bagg  
**Primary market:** Nigeria, with international and diaspora clients as an important expansion market  
**Purpose:** Establish a shared definition of Bagg, its users, problems, workflows, capabilities, boundaries, and intended evolution before detailed product requirements are written.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Bagg is a client-engagement workspace for freelancers, independent professionals, consultants, and service businesses. It brings together the commercial and delivery activities that happen between agreeing to do work and completing that work successfully.

The initial product foundation focuses on helping service providers create professional quotes and invoices, communicate payment obligations, track what has been paid, follow up on outstanding amounts, and issue receipts.

The product should evolve beyond invoicing into a workspace that connects:

- What a client requests.
- What the service provider agrees to deliver.
- How the engagement is structured and priced.
- What work is in progress.
- What the client needs to review, decide, or approve.
- What changes to scope are agreed.
- When invoices and payment obligations arise.
- Which payments have been confirmed.
- When deliverables become available for download or handover.
- What remains outstanding before an engagement is closed.

Bagg should support both **one-off projects and ongoing service retainers from the beginning**. These are different engagement patterns, but they share a common foundation: an agreement, delivery obligations, client decisions, financial obligations, and an eventual outcome.

The central product proposition is:

> **Bagg helps service businesses agree on the work, deliver it professionally, and get paid—with less friction and less chasing.**

Bagg should not become a generic project-management application, a full accounting suite, or an all-purpose customer relationship management system. Its purpose is to connect commercial commitments, client engagement, delivery, and payment in one coherent experience.

## 2. Product Vision

### 2.1 Vision

Make Bagg the natural place where independent service providers and small service businesses manage the complete client engagement, from the first meaningful conversation to final payment and handover.

A service provider should not have to stitch together separate tools just to prepare a quote, explain the scope, collect a deposit, share a preview, obtain approval, request a final payment, release the files, and close the engagement.

Bagg should provide the shared workspace that connects those activities while allowing the actual work to remain in the specialist tools that professionals already use.

### 2.2 Mission

Reduce the administrative, commercial, and coordination burden of running a service business by making engagements easier to define, manage, deliver, and settle.

### 2.3 Product promise

Bagg should help users:

1. Look professional when engaging clients.
2. Establish clear expectations before work begins.
3. Keep the client informed throughout delivery.
4. Reduce misunderstandings about scope, revisions, approvals, and payment.
5. Automate routine administration without surrendering control.
6. Protect legitimate payment and delivery obligations.
7. Complete engagements with clear records of what was agreed, delivered, approved, and paid.

### 2.4 The product's organising idea

Bagg is **engagement-centred**, not merely invoice-centred or task-centred.

An engagement is the relationship-specific unit of work between a service provider and a client. It may be a one-off project, an ongoing retainer, or another defined service arrangement.

An engagement connects the commercial agreement, delivery plan, client interactions, financial obligations, and handover. Each of those remains a distinct part of the system, but the user experiences them as one connected workflow.

## 3. The Problem Bagg Solves

Independent professionals and small service businesses frequently coordinate their work across disconnected tools.

A typical engagement might involve:

- WhatsApp or email for client conversations.
- Word, Google Docs, or a design tool for preparing a proposal.
- A spreadsheet or invoicing application for billing.
- A bank account or payment processor for collections.
- Google Drive, Dropbox, or another service for files.
- Figma, Canva, Adobe applications, or a media-review platform for previews and feedback.
- A task manager or personal notes for deadlines.
- Manual reminders for outstanding invoices.
- Separate messages to confirm approval and hand over final files.

Each tool may work well individually. The problem is the lack of continuity between them.

A quote may promise three revision rounds, but the client and provider may lose track of how many have been used. A client may approve a design in a message that is difficult to locate later. An additional request may be completed without an agreed change in price. A final invoice may be paid, but the provider may forget to send the final files. Alternatively, the provider may release original files before receiving the payment required by the agreement.

Bagg should reduce these failures by maintaining a coherent record of the engagement and coordinating the actions that depend on it.

### 3.1 Core problems

**Commercial ambiguity:** Clients and providers may not share a sufficiently precise understanding of scope, price, timelines, revisions, and payment conditions.

**Administrative fragmentation:** Quotes, agreements, project updates, invoices, approvals, and file handovers are scattered across different tools.

**Payment friction:** Service providers spend time following up on unpaid invoices and determining whether payment has actually been received.

**Scope creep:** Additional work is requested without a corresponding decision on cost, timing, or contractual obligations.

**Delivery friction:** Clients struggle to find current previews, provide structured feedback, approve the correct version, or access final deliverables.

**Weak engagement records:** Important decisions and payment events may be difficult to reconstruct when a dispute arises.

**Repeated setup work:** Service providers repeatedly create similar quotes, milestone plans, deliverables, and payment schedules for comparable engagements.

## 4. Target Users

### 4.1 Primary users

Bagg is primarily designed for people who sell expertise, creative output, professional services, or defined deliverables.

**Independent freelancers**
- Designers and brand identity specialists.
- Web designers and developers.
- Writers, editors, and content creators.
- Photographers and videographers.
- Illustrators, animators, and other creative professionals.

**Independent professionals and consultants**
- Business and management consultants.
- Marketing and communications consultants.
- Professional advisers.
- Trainers, facilitators, and specialist service providers.

**Small service businesses**
- Creative studios and boutique agencies.
- Web and digital service firms.
- Marketing and content agencies.
- Small consultancies.
- Other owner-operated businesses delivering work for clients.

### 4.2 Secondary users

**Clients and buyers** are essential participants, even when they never subscribe to Bagg. They need to review quotes, understand commitments, provide information, approve deliverables, make payments, and access completed work.

**Collaborators and team members** may need to help deliver an engagement, manage client communication, review files, or administer billing.

**International clients** need a professional, reliable experience even when the service provider operates in Nigeria.

### 4.3 Initial market orientation

Bagg should be designed around the realities of Nigerian independent professionals and small service businesses, including mobile-first communication, bank transfers, local payment methods, and price sensitivity.

International clients and the Nigerian diaspora are important because service providers may sell across borders. Bagg should support international engagement without assuming that the domestic and international markets have identical willingness to pay, payment preferences, or operational requirements.

## 5. Core Jobs to Be Done

Bagg should enable a user to:

1. Turn a client's request into a clear proposal and commercial agreement.
2. Explain the price, scope, timelines, revisions, and payment conditions.
3. Obtain client acceptance and any required formal agreement.
4. Organise the work into meaningful milestones and deliverables.
5. Collect deposits and scheduled payments.
6. Show the client what is happening without excessive manual updates.
7. Share previews and collect feedback on specific versions.
8. Record approvals and distinguish them from informal comments.
9. Handle additional requests through an explicit scope-change process.
10. Generate invoices when agreed conditions are satisfied.
11. Confirm payments and issue receipts.
12. Release files or handover materials when the agreed conditions are met.
13. Manage recurring service arrangements without recreating the entire engagement every month.
14. Reuse proven workflows for similar work.
15. Understand what requires attention across clients, projects, delivery, and collections.

## 6. Product Principles

### 6.1 Commercially grounded delivery

Project and milestone functionality exists to support service delivery and commercial commitments. Bagg should not accumulate generic project-management features without a clear connection to the needs of a service business.

### 6.2 Clear separation of business facts

Bagg must distinguish:

- A quote from an accepted agreement.
- A task from a milestone.
- A deliverable from its approval.
- Approval from payment.
- An invoice from a confirmed settlement.
- Payment from final handover.
- A requested change from an agreed change.

These concepts may be connected through rules, but they must not be treated as interchangeable.

### 6.3 Explicit agreements

The system should preserve what was agreed, including scope, price, revision allowances, payment schedules, and applicable delivery conditions. Material changes should be recorded rather than silently overwriting the original agreement.

### 6.4 Client experience matters

Clients should be able to complete common actions through a clear, professional link or workspace without being forced to learn the provider's internal tools or create an account unnecessarily.

### 6.5 Integrate rather than replace

Bagg should orchestrate the engagement and provide a coherent client experience. It should not try to replace every design application, file-storage provider, media-review tool, or development platform.

### 6.6 Automate routine work, retain control

Reminders, invoice generation, status updates, and eligible file releases should be automated when their conditions are clear. Financial confirmation, contractual exceptions, and other consequential actions must have appropriate controls.

### 6.7 Designed for one-off and recurring work

One-off projects and retainers must both be supported as first-class engagement types. The system should not assume that every client relationship ends after one delivery or that every engagement has a fixed end date.

### 6.8 Simple by default, configurable when necessary

Common workflows should be easy to start. Advanced configuration should be available when a service provider needs different approval, billing, revision, or delivery rules.

## 7. The Core Product Model

The following concepts form the intended product model. They describe the product domain, not a claim that the underlying data structures already exist.

### 7.1 Client

A person or organisation purchasing services.

A client record should contain enough information to support engagement history, quotes, agreements, invoices, payment records, and communication. Bagg should not attempt to become a comprehensive CRM.

### 7.2 Engagement

The overarching relationship-specific unit of work. An engagement has a client, a service provider, a commercial basis, a lifecycle, and a collection of associated records.

Engagement types include:

- One-off project.
- Ongoing retainer.
- Other structured service arrangement where justified.

### 7.3 Quote or proposal

A proposed commercial arrangement describing the work, price, assumptions, deliverables, timelines, and payment conditions.

Quotes may be revised before acceptance. Material changes after acceptance should follow an agreed change process.

### 7.4 Agreement

The accepted commercial terms governing the engagement. Depending on the service and risk, this may be represented by an accepted quote, a separate contract, or both.

A contract-signing capability may be developed or integrated where necessary. The product definition does not assume that legally sufficient electronic signature functionality already exists.

### 7.5 Delivery plan

The structure through which work is organised. It may contain milestones, tasks, deliverables, review stages, and deadlines.

A delivery plan should support the commercial agreement without requiring every internal task to be visible to the client.

### 7.6 Milestone

A meaningful stage of the engagement, such as concept approval, first draft, development completion, or final handover.

A milestone may have a due date, deliverables, approval conditions, and billing rules. Not every milestone necessarily generates an invoice.

### 7.7 Deliverable

A piece of work promised to the client, such as a design, document, website, video, report, or source-file package.

A deliverable may have multiple versions, preview and download permissions, feedback, approval status, and release conditions.

### 7.8 Invoice and payment

An invoice records a payment obligation. A payment record captures a payment event and its verified status. A receipt provides evidence of a recognised payment.

An invoice may be partially paid, fully paid, overdue, or otherwise outstanding. The financial record must not rely solely on a client clicking a confirmation button.

### 7.9 Client decision

A meaningful decision made by the client, including quote acceptance, scope-change acceptance, deliverable approval, or a documented rejection/request for revision.

Decisions should reference the relevant object and version where applicable.

### 7.10 Handover

The final transfer of agreed materials, access, documentation, and other completion items. Handover may depend on approval and payment conditions, but those conditions must reflect the agreement.

## 8. End-to-End Engagement Lifecycle

The lifecycle below is the principal organising workflow for Bagg. Not every engagement needs every step.

### Stage 1: Initial request and qualification

The service provider receives a request from a potential or existing client.

Bagg should offer a lightweight way to capture the essential information needed to define the work. This may include the client's objectives, requirements, budget range, deadline, reference materials, and relevant constraints.

The intake experience should be configurable by service type, but Bagg should avoid becoming a broad lead-generation or sales CRM.

### Stage 2: Quote preparation

The provider creates a quote using a blank form, a reusable template, a service package, or AI-assisted drafting.

The quote should support:
- Line items and descriptions.
- Quantities, rates, and amounts.
- Discounts where supported.
- Scope and deliverables.
- Assumptions and exclusions.
- Timelines and estimated duration.
- Included revision rounds.
- Deposit and subsequent payment conditions.
- Quote expiry where relevant.
- Supporting attachments or reference material.

The provider can share a professional quote link or export it in a suitable format.

### Stage 3: Review, revision, and acceptance

The client reviews the quote and may accept it, decline it, or request changes.

Bagg should preserve the relevant quote version and acceptance record. A material change before acceptance should create or preserve an identifiable revision.

Once accepted, the agreed commercial terms should become the baseline for delivery and billing.

### Stage 4: Formal agreement, where required

Some engagements need a separate contract or additional formal acceptance.

Bagg should support a clear relationship between the quote, accepted terms, and any separate agreement. A contract may refer to the quote and define additional obligations, confidentiality, intellectual property, cancellation, or other terms.

The exact legal-signature mechanism and jurisdictional requirements remain subject to separate product and legal decisions.

### Stage 5: Engagement setup

Bagg creates or activates the engagement using the accepted terms.

The provider can configure:
- Milestones and target dates.
- Deliverables.
- Responsibilities.
- Review stages.
- Payment schedule.
- Invoice-generation conditions.
- Client-visible and internal information.
- File-release and handover conditions.

The system should generate a useful default structure from the accepted quote or template without requiring extensive manual recreation.

### Stage 6: Deposit or initial payment

If the agreement requires an upfront payment, Bagg presents the obligation and available payment instructions.

The engagement can show whether the payment is required, pending, confirmed, partially received, or overdue. The provider should be able to understand what is required before delivery begins.

Whether work can begin before a deposit is paid should be configurable according to the agreement and provider's operating rules.

### Stage 7: Work and delivery coordination

The provider manages delivery through the engagement workspace.

This includes relevant milestones, deliverables, due dates, internal tasks, progress updates, client-visible status, and linked assets.

The client should be able to understand what has been delivered, what needs attention, and what happens next without being exposed to unnecessary internal details.

### Stage 8: Preview, feedback, and revision

The provider shares a preview or reviewable version of a deliverable.

The client can submit feedback, request changes, or approve the relevant version. Feedback should remain associated with the deliverable and version it concerns.

Where the quote includes a defined number of revision rounds, Bagg should help both parties understand the allowance and record usage.

A request that exceeds the agreed scope should be eligible for the scope-change workflow rather than being silently treated as included work.

### Stage 9: Scope change, when needed

Either party may identify a material change in requirements, deliverables, deadlines, or assumptions.

Bagg should support a change request that describes:
- What is changing.
- Why it is changing.
- The effect on price.
- The effect on deadlines and milestones.
- Any effect on payment or deliverables.
- The client's decision.

Once accepted, the change should become part of the engagement's commercial and delivery history. The original agreement and the approved change should remain reconstructable.

### Stage 10: Milestone completion and billing

When an agreed billing condition is met, Bagg can create or prompt the creation of the relevant invoice.

Possible triggers include:
- A scheduled date.
- Completion of a milestone.
- Submission of a deliverable.
- Client approval.
- A recurring billing period.
- A manually confirmed event where the agreement requires it.

The trigger must be configurable. Approval should not automatically imply payment is due unless that is how the agreement defines the billing event.

### Stage 11: Payment confirmation and reconciliation

The client pays using the available payment method.

Bagg updates the payment status only after receiving sufficient evidence from a trusted provider, verified bank evidence, or an authorised reconciliation process.

Where a payment is partial, the outstanding balance should remain visible. Duplicate webhook events must not create duplicate payment records or receipts.

### Stage 12: Payment-conditioned release

Where the agreement requires payment before release, Bagg evaluates the configured release conditions.

For example, release may require both approval of the final deliverable and confirmed settlement of the final invoice.

When all conditions are satisfied, Bagg unlocks the authorised files or activates the appropriate access links. It records what was released, to whom, when, and under which conditions.

The system should not assume that every service requires payment-gated files. The release policy belongs to the engagement.

### Stage 13: Final handover and closure

Bagg presents a completion view that identifies the deliverables, approvals, invoices, payments, and handover items relevant to closure.

Depending on the agreement, handover may include source files, final exports, credentials transfer, documentation, deployment information, or support instructions.

Bagg should distinguish completed work from outstanding obligations. It must not silently make every handover contingent on payment if the accepted agreement says otherwise.

### Stage 14: Ongoing relationship

After closure, the client may engage the provider again, extend the work, begin a new project, or move into a recurring retainer.

Bagg should make it easy to reuse appropriate client information and templates while creating a distinct record for the new engagement.

## 9. One-Off Projects and Retainers

Both engagement types are in scope from the beginning.

### 9.1 One-off projects

A one-off project generally has a defined scope, an intended outcome, and a completion or handover point.

It may contain multiple milestones, deliverables, revisions, invoices, and payments. The project can have a fixed price, time-based charges, or another agreed commercial structure.

### 9.2 Ongoing retainers

A retainer is a continuing arrangement under which a client pays for an agreed period of access, capacity, services, or recurring deliverables.

Examples include:
- Monthly design support.
- Website maintenance.
- Ongoing marketing and content services.
- Advisory or consulting retainers.
- Recurring development support.

Retainers should support:
- Billing frequency and period.
- Service or deliverable allowances.
- Included work and excess-work rules.
- Renewal and termination conditions.
- Period-specific delivery records.
- Changes in scope, price, and service level.
- Carryover or expiry rules where contractually applicable.
- Payment status and collection follow-up.

A retainer must not be treated as one endless project with no meaningful periods. It needs a continuing relationship record and identifiable periods of service, billing, and delivery.

### 9.3 Shared foundation

Both engagement types should reuse the same core concepts: client, agreement, delivery obligations, decisions, invoices, payments, and records.

The user experience should adapt to the engagement type rather than duplicating the entire product into separate project and retainer systems.

## 10. Feature Catalogue

The following catalogue defines the intended product capability areas. It is a scope definition, not a claim that every listed capability is implemented today.

### 10.1 Client and engagement management

**Purpose:** Give the provider a reliable view of each client relationship and its active work.

Capabilities:
- Client records and contact details.
- Client engagement history.
- Linked quotes, agreements, projects, retainers, invoices, and receipts.
- Engagement status and important dates.
- Notes and relevant reference material.
- Multiple engagements for the same client.
- Client-facing access without exposing unrelated client records.
- Search and filtering across engagements.

Boundary: This is engagement-oriented client management, not a full sales CRM with extensive lead pipelines, marketing automation, and unrelated sales operations.

### 10.2 Quotes and proposals

**Purpose:** Make it easy to present a professional offer and establish clear expectations.

Capabilities:
- Quote creation and editing.
- Line items, quantities, rates, discounts, and totals.
- Scope, assumptions, exclusions, and deliverables.
- Revision allowances and timing.
- Payment terms and schedules.
- Expiry dates and quote status.
- Shareable client view.
- PDF or other suitable export.
- Quote versions and acceptance history.
- Reusable service packages and templates.
- Conversion into an engagement after acceptance.

### 10.3 Agreements and contracts

**Purpose:** Preserve the accepted terms governing the engagement.

Capabilities to define:
- Accepted quote as the commercial baseline.
- Optional separate contract.
- Contract templates and service-specific clauses.
- Acceptance and signature records.
- Version history.
- Links between contract terms and delivery/payment rules.
- Renewal, extension, and termination for continuing engagements.

Electronic signatures, legal enforceability, and jurisdiction-specific requirements require explicit validation before those capabilities are represented as legally sufficient.

### 10.4 Engagement and delivery management

**Purpose:** Coordinate work without turning Bagg into a generic task-management suite.

Capabilities:
- Engagement creation from an accepted quote or template.
- Milestones and deadlines.
- Deliverables linked to milestones.
- Internal tasks where they materially support delivery.
- Internal and client-visible status.
- Progress summaries.
- Dependencies where useful.
- Assignment of work to collaborators.
- Completion and handover tracking.
- A clear view of next actions and blocked items.

The product should prioritise client delivery and commercial commitments over generic productivity features such as extensive project boards, resource optimisation, or enterprise portfolio planning.

### 10.5 Deliverables, files, and versions

**Purpose:** Connect the work being delivered to the client's review, approval, and handover experience.

Capabilities:
- Deliverable records.
- Links to externally stored assets.
- Supported previews or embedded views.
- Version history or identifiable review versions.
- Download permissions.
- Client feedback associated with a version.
- Approval records.
- Final-file bundles.
- Release conditions.
- Handover checklists.

Bagg should distinguish the deliverable record from the file itself. A deliverable may reference one or several files, and a file may be stored in an external system.

### 10.6 Client workspace and portal

**Purpose:** Give the client a coherent place to participate in the engagement.

The client experience should support, as applicable:
- Reviewing a quote.
- Accepting or declining a proposal.
- Viewing relevant agreement details.
- Seeing delivery status.
- Accessing previews.
- Providing feedback.
- Requesting or accepting changes.
- Approving deliverables.
- Viewing invoices and payment instructions.
- Confirming payment through supported flows.
- Downloading authorised final files.
- Accessing handover materials.

The experience should be mobile-friendly and easy to access through secure links or authenticated access, depending on the sensitivity of the action and information.

### 10.7 Feedback, revisions, and approvals

**Purpose:** Prevent decisions from being lost in scattered conversations.

Capabilities:
- Comments and feedback on supported deliverables.
- Association of feedback with a specific version.
- Revision requests.
- Approval and rejection actions.
- Approval history.
- Revision allowances and usage.
- Clear distinction between comments, requests, and formal approval.
- Notification to the provider when a decision is required.
- Approval records that can be referenced during billing or handover.

External design and media tools may retain their own commenting systems. Bagg should integrate or link to them where appropriate rather than pretending all external comments can always be imported.

### 10.8 Scope and change management

**Purpose:** Protect both parties from uncontrolled changes.

Capabilities:
- Change requests initiated by either party.
- Description of requested changes.
- Impact on price, delivery dates, milestones, and payment.
- Client approval or rejection.
- Updated commercial terms.
- Traceable relationship between the original agreement and approved changes.
- Revision allowances that distinguish included revisions from additional work.

This is a high-priority differentiating capability because it connects a common service-business problem directly to commercial outcomes.

### 10.9 Invoicing and financial obligations

**Purpose:** Make billing accurate, timely, and connected to the agreement.

The initial product foundation includes professional quote and invoice workflows, payment-status visibility, follow-ups, and receipts as described in the existing product concept. The precise implementation status of individual capabilities must be verified separately.

The intended capability set includes:
- Invoice creation.
- Invoice numbering and client details.
- Line items, currency, totals, and applicable charges.
- Invoice due dates and payment instructions.
- Links between invoices and engagements or milestones.
- Deposit invoices and staged billing.
- Recurring invoices for retainers.
- Partial payments and outstanding balances.
- Payment reminders and follow-ups.
- Receipts for recognised payments.
- Invoice status and collection visibility.
- Invoice export and shareable links.
- Appropriate records of changes and payment allocations.

Bagg should not assume that an invoice has been paid simply because it was viewed, marked by a client as paid, or followed by a browser redirect.

### 10.10 Payment confirmation and collection

**Purpose:** Reduce manual reconciliation and uncertainty around money received.

Capabilities to define:
- Supported payment methods and provider integrations.
- Payment-status updates from trusted events.
- Webhook handling with authentication and replay protection.
- Duplicate-event handling.
- Partial-payment support.
- Reconciliation and authorised manual confirmation.
- Payment allocation to the appropriate obligation.
- Receipts and confirmation messages.
- Failed or reversed payment handling where supported.
- Outstanding-balance visibility.

The product should distinguish a payment initiated, a payment reported successful, and a payment sufficiently confirmed for the action that depends on it. The required degree of confirmation may differ by provider and payment rail.

### 10.11 Payment-conditioned deliverable access

**Purpose:** Let providers release deliverables according to the commercial terms of the engagement.

This should be a first-class capability within delivery and billing.

Capabilities:
- Configurable release conditions.
- Approval requirements.
- Required payment amount or obligation.
- Selection of the deliverables or files governed by the condition.
- Automatic release after all required conditions are satisfied.
- Verified payment events as triggers.
- Controlled access to downloads or external file links.
- Release notifications.
- Release audit history.
- Authorised exceptions where permitted.
- Clear client-facing explanations of what remains locked and why.

Possible policies include:
- Preview available immediately; final download after full payment.
- A milestone bundle released after the associated invoice is settled.
- Approved deliverables released after a specified payment threshold.
- Immediate release where the agreement does not require payment gating.

**Security requirement:** Access controls must be enforced by the actual file-delivery mechanism. Hiding a link in the interface is insufficient if a client can still access a public or permanent original-file URL.

Bagg should support external storage only to the degree that the integration can reliably enforce or represent the agreed access conditions. It must disclose when an external provider cannot guarantee the desired restriction.

### 10.12 Handover and engagement closure

**Purpose:** Ensure that completed work is handed over clearly and the engagement can be closed with confidence.

Capabilities:
- Final deliverable checklist.
- Required approvals.
- Required payments, where specified by the agreement.
- File and access transfer.
- Documentation and handover notes.
- Completion summary.
- Record of outstanding obligations.
- Optional support or warranty period.
- Clear closure status.
- Ability to reopen or extend an engagement through an explicit action.

The system must not conflate “work completed,” “invoice settled,” and “engagement closed.” These may occur at different times.

### 10.13 Recurring services and retainers

**Purpose:** Support repeatable service delivery and recurring revenue.

Capabilities:
- Retainer setup.
- Billing period and renewal configuration.
- Recurring invoice schedules.
- Period-specific service and delivery records.
- Included allowance and excess-work rules.
- Renewal reminders.
- Price and scope changes.
- Cancellation and termination records.
- Carryover or expiry rules where agreed.
- Visibility into upcoming billing and obligations.

### 10.14 Templates and service packages

**Purpose:** Reduce setup time and make good service-business practices repeatable.

Capabilities:
- Quote templates.
- Service packages.
- Engagement templates.
- Milestone and deliverable templates.
- Revision policies.
- Payment schedules.
- Review and approval stages.
- Handover checklists.
- Retainer templates.
- Reuse of previous engagement structures without unintentionally copying confidential client information.

Templates should be adaptable. Bagg should not force every provider in a service category into the same workflow.

### 10.15 Notifications and communications

**Purpose:** Ensure the right person knows when action is required.

Potential notifications include:
- Quote sent, viewed, accepted, declined, or expiring.
- Deposit or invoice due.
- Payment overdue.
- Payment confirmed.
- Work ready for review.
- Feedback received.
- Approval requested or recorded.
- Scope change awaiting a decision.
- Milestone due or delayed.
- Files released.
- Handover outstanding.
- Retainer renewal or recurring billing event.

Supported channels may include email and other appropriate communication channels such as shareable links and messaging integrations. Channel availability must be validated; Bagg should not assume every channel is already integrated.

Notifications should be configurable to prevent excessive messaging and should link to the relevant action.

### 10.16 Dashboard and operational overview

**Purpose:** Give the provider an actionable view of work and money.

The dashboard should prioritise:
- Outstanding invoices and overdue amounts.
- Expected payments and upcoming billing.
- Quotes awaiting client decisions.
- Engagements at risk of delay.
- Deliverables awaiting review or approval.
- Scope changes awaiting acceptance.
- Work blocked by a required client action.
- Payment-confirmed files awaiting release, where applicable.
- Handover items outstanding.
- Upcoming retainer renewals and billing periods.

Reports should help the provider understand operational and financial performance without requiring a full accounting system.

### 10.17 AI assistance

**Purpose:** Reduce administrative effort and help providers run engagements more professionally.

Potential capabilities:
- Draft a quote from a client's brief.
- Suggest scope, assumptions, exclusions, and clarifying questions.
- Recommend relevant templates and service packages.
- Propose milestones, deliverables, and payment schedules.
- Summarise client feedback and identify conflicting requests.
- Draft progress updates and payment reminders.
- Identify possible scope creep.
- Suggest a change request when new work appears outside the agreement.
- Summarise outstanding client decisions.
- Prepare handover summaries.
- Explain the state of an engagement in natural language.

AI-generated commercial terms, prices, commitments, and scope changes should require appropriate review. AI must not fabricate client approvals, confirm unverified payments, or release protected files on its own without satisfying the applicable deterministic rules.

### 10.18 Integrations

**Purpose:** Let providers continue using specialist tools while Bagg maintains engagement continuity.

Potential integration categories include:

**File storage**
- Google Drive.
- Microsoft OneDrive.
- Dropbox.
- Box.

**Design and creative tools**
- Figma.
- Canva.
- Adobe services where supported.

**Media review**
- Video and media-review platforms that support suitable sharing or integration.

**Development and deployment**
- GitHub or GitLab.
- Website hosting and deployment services where the workflow warrants it.

**Payments**
- Relevant Nigerian payment providers and supported bank/payment rails.
- International payment options as commercially and technically appropriate.

**Communication**
- Email and relevant messaging channels.

Integration depth will vary. Some providers may support embedded previews; others may only support links, authentication, or limited metadata. Bagg must represent those differences honestly and never promise a capability the external provider does not permit.

### 10.19 Account, workspace, and access management

**Purpose:** Keep client information and commercial records appropriately separated.

Capabilities to define:
- Provider account and workspace setup.
- Team members and roles where needed.
- Client-specific access.
- Internal versus client-visible information.
- Permission controls for financial actions and deliverable release.
- Secure share links and authenticated access as appropriate.
- Activity history for important events.
- Data export and account lifecycle controls.

Security and privacy requirements should be designed into the product rather than added after integrations and client portals are established.

### 10.20 Onboarding and setup

**Purpose:** Help providers reach a useful first outcome quickly.

Onboarding should establish the provider's identity, business details, invoice defaults, currency and payment instructions, service categories, and preferred workflow.

Bagg may recommend templates and defaults based on the provider's work. Setup should remain lightweight and allow advanced settings to be introduced when needed.

## 11. The Payment and Delivery Orchestration Model

This is a core product differentiator and deserves explicit treatment.

Bagg should coordinate business events without collapsing them into a single status.

For example:

- A deliverable may be ready for review but not approved.
- A deliverable may be approved but not yet paid for.
- An invoice may be issued but not yet due.
- A payment may be initiated but not confirmed.
- A payment may be confirmed while some other invoice remains outstanding.
- A deliverable may be released while an unrelated obligation remains open.
- Work may be complete while handover or documentation remains outstanding.

The system should evaluate configured rules against these separate facts.

A typical release rule might be:

> Release the final source files only when the required final deliverable version has been approved and the specified payment obligation has been confirmed as settled.

The system should be able to explain why a release has or has not occurred.

This model should also support other combinations: invoice generation after milestone completion, approval-triggered billing, scheduled retainer invoices, and reminders after a due date.

The guiding rule is that **automation follows the agreement and verified events**, not assumptions about what normally happens in a project.

## 12. Product Boundaries

Bagg should resist feature expansion that weakens its central proposition.

### 12.1 Not a full accounting system

Bagg should handle quotes, invoices, payments, receipts, and engagement-related financial visibility. It should not initially attempt to replace a general ledger, statutory accounting suite, payroll system, or comprehensive tax-accounting platform.

### 12.2 Not a generic CRM

Client records and engagement history are necessary. A broad lead-generation, sales-pipeline, campaign-management, or marketing-automation suite is not part of the core product.

### 12.3 Not a general-purpose project-management platform

Tasks and milestones exist to support client delivery and commercial commitments. Advanced resource planning, portfolio management, and unrelated internal productivity features should not drive the roadmap without a clear user need.

### 12.4 Not a replacement for creative tools

Bagg should not recreate Figma, Canva, Adobe, video editing, or development environments. It should connect the outputs of those tools to the client engagement.

### 12.5 Not a universal file-storage provider

Bagg may store certain files or coordinate access to external assets. It should not assume that it must host every source file or media asset itself.

### 12.6 Not a payment processor

Bagg may integrate with payment providers and bank/payment rails, but it should not claim to process or settle payments itself unless the relevant capability, approvals, and operating arrangements exist.

## 13. Pricing and Commercial Model

Pricing should reflect the value Bagg creates in professional presentation, administrative efficiency, delivery coordination, and reliable collection—not simply the number of invoices generated.

The product should support a free entry point and paid tiers that create a clear progression as users require more capability.

### 13.1 Pricing principles

- Make it easy for a new freelancer to try Bagg.
- Ensure paid tiers deliver meaningful operational value.
- Avoid pricing that punishes healthy usage of the core workflow.
- Consider the different willingness to pay in Nigeria and international markets.
- Charge for meaningful increases in value, such as advanced workflows, team collaboration, or higher-value automation.
- Make restrictions understandable and visible before users depend on a feature.
- Avoid making core data access or financial records hostage to an unexpected upgrade.

### 13.2 Illustrative pricing hypothesis

The following figures are planning hypotheses, not validated or final prices.

| Tier | Illustrative annual price in Nigeria | Intended role |
|---|---:|---|
| Free | ₦0 | Entry-level invoicing and discovery |
| Starter | ₦75,000 | Independent provider with regular client work |
| Professional | ₦150,000 | Provider managing multiple engagements and more advanced workflows |
| Studio | ₦300,000 | Small service business requiring collaboration and broader operational controls |

A separate international pricing schedule should be researched and tested rather than derived mechanically from currency conversion. Previously considered international examples include $120, $250, and $590 annually for paid tiers, but those figures also remain unvalidated.

The exact feature limits, payment terms, international pricing, and plan entitlements should be settled through product research and commercial testing before implementation.

## 14. Success Measures

Bagg should measure whether users successfully manage engagements, deliver work, and collect money—not merely whether they create records.

### 14.1 Activation

- Time from signup to first useful quote or invoice.
- Percentage of new users who send a quote or invoice.
- Percentage who complete a first client engagement.
- Time to first client acceptance or payment.

### 14.2 Engagement adoption

- Percentage of quotes converted into engagements.
- Percentage of active engagements using milestones or deliverables.
- Percentage of engagements with client-facing activity.
- Adoption of recurring retainers.
- Repeat engagements from existing clients.

### 14.3 Financial outcomes

- Invoices issued and settled.
- Time from invoice issuance to confirmed payment.
- Outstanding and overdue balances.
- Percentage of payments matched to the correct obligations.
- Frequency of manual payment follow-up.
- Adoption and success rate of supported payment integrations.

### 14.4 Delivery outcomes

- Time from work submission to client decision.
- Time spent waiting for client feedback or approval.
- Frequency of unapproved scope changes.
- Percentage of engagements with recorded handover.
- Percentage of eligible file releases completed automatically.
- Incidents involving premature or incorrectly restricted file access.

### 14.5 Retention and commercial health

- Weekly and monthly active providers.
- Retention by cohort.
- Paid conversion.
- Upgrade and downgrade rates.
- Revenue per paying workspace.
- Retainer usage and renewal.
- Referral and repeat-client activity.

These measures should be interpreted together. For example, more invoices created is not necessarily success if payment collection and user retention do not improve.

## 15. Roadmap Direction

This roadmap is a product-sequencing proposal, not a delivery commitment.

### Phase 1: Establish the engagement foundation

- Define a consistent engagement model for one-off projects and retainers.
- Connect quotes, accepted terms, delivery structures, invoices, and payments.
- Establish milestones, deliverables, and client-facing engagement status.
- Provide clear records of client decisions.
- Define the core financial and delivery state transitions.

### Phase 2: Make client delivery professional

- Client-facing engagement workspace.
- Deliverable previews and version-specific feedback.
- Formal approvals.
- Revision allowances.
- Handover checklist and completion records.
- File access permissions and controlled download workflows.

### Phase 3: Orchestrate scope, billing, and release

- Formal scope-change requests and acceptance.
- Configurable billing triggers.
- Deposit and staged-payment workflows.
- Payment confirmation integrations.
- Payment-conditioned deliverable release.
- Auditable release and exception handling.

### Phase 4: Make repeat work easy

- Service packages and reusable templates.
- Retainer billing and period-based delivery.
- Renewal and scope-change workflows.
- Repeat-engagement setup.
- Better operational dashboards and collection forecasts.

### Phase 5: Add deeper integrations and AI

- More capable storage, design, media, and development integrations.
- AI-assisted quoting and engagement setup.
- Feedback and scope-change summaries.
- Contextual reminders and progress updates.
- Workflow recommendations and controlled automation.

The phases are not rigid. The final order should depend on current implementation status, customer evidence, engineering constraints, and the smallest coherent release that provides meaningful value.

## 16. Risks and Design Considerations

### 16.1 Scope expansion

Connecting quotes, projects, files, payments, and retainers can create a broad product. The safeguard is to require each capability to support a core engagement workflow.

### 16.2 External integration limitations

Providers differ in permissions, authentication, embedding, APIs, and file-release controls. Integration capabilities must be defined per provider rather than assumed to be uniform.

### 16.3 Payment certainty

A webhook can report a successful transaction, but its meaning depends on the payment provider and payment rail. Bagg must validate event authenticity and understand whether the event represents initiation, authorisation, receipt, settlement, reversal, or another state.

### 16.4 Incorrect release decisions

Automatically releasing the wrong file, releasing too early, or blocking access after contractual conditions have been met can cause material harm. Release policies need deterministic evaluation, reliable payment evidence, and auditable exceptions.

### 16.5 Contractual and legal variation

Deposits, intellectual property, cancellation, late fees, approvals, and file handover can have different contractual and legal implications. Bagg should support configurable commercial terms and avoid making universal legal claims.

### 16.6 Over-automation

AI should help draft, summarise, and recommend. It should not independently invent contractual commitments, treat ambiguous feedback as approval, or bypass payment and access controls.

### 16.7 Client friction

A sophisticated provider workspace is not enough. Clients must be able to review, approve, pay, and obtain authorised files without unnecessary complexity.

## 17. Assumptions and Open Decisions

The following items require confirmation or further investigation before a detailed PRD is finalised.

1. **Current implementation baseline:** Which invoicing, quoting, payment tracking, reminders, receipts, projects, and milestone capabilities are implemented and working today?
2. **Contract handling:** Should Bagg provide native contract creation and signature workflows, integrate with an external service, or support both over time?
3. **Payment integrations:** Which Nigerian and international payment providers should be supported first?
4. **Payment confirmation:** What confirmation standards are required for each provider and payment rail?
5. **External file integrations:** Which storage and creative platforms have sufficient user demand and suitable access-control capabilities for the first release?
6. **Client authentication:** Which actions can be performed through secure links, and which require a client account or stronger authentication?
7. **Retainer billing:** Which billing frequencies and allowance models are essential for the initial release?
8. **Team collaboration:** Which roles and permissions are required for individual providers versus studios and agencies?
9. **Tax and invoicing requirements:** What local and international invoice fields, records, and compliance requirements are in scope?
10. **Pricing:** Which tier entitlements and price points are supported by customer research and willingness-to-pay testing?
11. **File release exceptions:** Who can authorise exceptions, which controls can be overridden, and what evidence must be retained?
12. **Cancellation and disputes:** What happens to deposits, partially completed work, approvals, and file access when an engagement is cancelled or disputed?

## 18. Definition of Product Success

Bagg succeeds when a service provider can use it to manage a real client engagement with less administrative effort and greater commercial clarity.

A representative successful journey is:

A provider receives a brief, creates and sends a quote, secures acceptance, sets up the engagement, collects the agreed deposit, coordinates work and reviews, handles any scope changes, invoices according to the agreement, confirms payment, releases the appropriate files, and records the handover.

The same provider can also manage a monthly retainer without rebuilding the engagement from scratch every billing period.

The client experiences a coherent, professional process. The provider retains a clear record of what was agreed, what was delivered, what was approved, what was paid, and what remains outstanding.

Bagg should make that experience easier, more reliable, and more repeatable than managing the engagement across disconnected tools.

## 19. Final Product Definition

**Bagg is an engagement-centred workspace for service providers that connects quoting, agreements, client decisions, delivery, invoicing, payment confirmation, and handover.**

It begins with helping service providers invoice professionally and get paid. It grows into the place where they manage the entire commercial relationship around the work they deliver.

Its differentiation should come from connecting the parts of service delivery that are normally disconnected: explicit scope, client approval, payment obligations, and controlled handover.

Bagg should be powerful enough to manage real service-business workflows, but focused enough that users can understand it quickly and adopt it without changing the way they create their work.

The guiding product principle is:

**Agree on the work. Deliver it clearly. Confirm the money. Complete the engagement.**
